We've built good software that went nowhere, and [SendTrucks](https://sendtrucks.com/) is probably the best example. It connected businesses that needed dump trucks with truckers looking for work. Businesses could post loads, truckers could find them, and both sides could handle dispatching, routing, and receipts in one place.

We were proud of the product we built, but it never got the traction we expected. Our natural response was to look at the product for answers. We wondered if onboarding was too complicated, the screens were confusing, or if we were missing some feature that would finally convince people to use it. None of those things explained what was happening.

## The truckers came

We had a line of truckers willing to subscribe, and their interest went beyond telling us it was a neat idea. They were willing to pay because an idle truck isn't making money and access to more loads meant more opportunities to put that truck to work.

We also weren't guessing at an industry from a distance. Elisha Haan brought industry experience to our partnership after hauling for many of the large companies we approached. His experience gave us a legitimate way into conversations with the exact businesses we needed.

Even with those connections, we could barely get anyone to post loads. Posting was free, but a marketplace full of truckers with no loads is worthless. You'd think free would make posting an easy sell, but price had very little to do with why businesses resisted it.

## We were disrupting more than software

The businesses posting loads already had processes involving phone calls, text messages, paper, or a list of the same people they always called. We looked at that and saw an inefficient process that software could clean up, but the process already worked for the businesses using it.

It also involved relationships. Work historically went to people they knew, including friends, long-time partners, or simply someone they trusted to answer the phone and show up. Opening a load to a “free market” meant some random trucker could bid on work that previously went to someone with an existing relationship to the business. We thought that openness was part of the value, while many of the people we needed saw it as a threat to how they did business.

Asking them to post a load meant asking them to change how they handed out work, risk existing relationships, and give up some control over who got an opportunity. Truckers had everything to gain from opening the market, but the businesses posting loads did not. That imbalance seems painfully obvious to us now, but we were too focused on whether the product worked to fully appreciate it.

## Good product, bad business

We don't think SendTrucks was a bad product. Our mistake was giving the quality of the product too much credit because the software was the part we could most directly control. There is always another feature to add, edge case to fix, or workflow to polish, and all of it feels like progress because the changes are visible.

None of that work could fix incentives that did not line up. Good code did not give businesses a reason to post loads, make the change urgent, or make them comfortable disrupting relationships.

That's the part we missed. We saw an inefficient process and assumed it was waiting for better software. In reality, the process was doing exactly what the people involved wanted it to do.

Now, before we build, we want to know who has to change and what they stand to lose. SendTrucks depended on businesses voluntarily opening a mostly closed relationship network. We should have treated that as a fundamental risk instead of an adoption problem we'd solve after building the product.

The title of this post isn't entirely accurate because the truckers did come, but without businesses posting loads there was nothing for them to come for and none of the rest mattered.
